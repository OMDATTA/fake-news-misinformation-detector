        Not a fact-check. A high score means the writing resembles articles from sites that were flagged as unreliable in 2015–2018. Accurate reporting can be written in a sensational style, and false claims can be written in a sober one. Verify claims with primary sources and professional fact-checkers.

        How the estimate is made

        A deliberately transparent model: every number it uses can be inspected.

        1. 01

          Scrub publisher fingerprints

          Remove “Reuters”, datelines, image credits, embedded tweets, URLs, “[VIDEO]” tags and timestamps. On their own these markers predict the label with 96% accuracy, so leaving them in would teach the model to recognise publishers, not language.

        2. 02

          TF-IDF n-grams

          25,000 word and two-word phrases, sublinear term frequency, L2-normalised.

        3. 03

          15 linguistic features

          Sentence length, ALL-CAPS share, exclamation and question rates, quotation and attribution density, sensational and hedging vocabulary, readability and more, all standardised.

        4. 04

          Class-weighted logistic regression

          Linear, so each phrase’s push toward either class is exactly its weight × its TF-IDF value. That is what the highlights show.

        5. 05

          Platt calibration

          A sigmoid fitted on a separate calibration split, so “80%” means roughly 80 in 100 similar test articles were misleading.

        Dense embeddings capture topic more than style here, and they do not generalise better. The lighter TF-IDF + linguistic model is what ships.

        Class imbalance

        Real feeds contain far less misinformation than ISOT’s ~45%. Here train and test are resampled to 10% misleading.

        StrategyPrecisionRecallF1PR-AUC

        Accuracy is misleading under imbalance; PR-AUC and recall on the minority class are reported instead. Class weighting trades a little precision for recall.

        What this tool is not

        It is a style-resemblance model. It never sees the claims’ truth value, the outside world, or anything after 2018.

        • Labels are about sources, not claims. ISOT marks every article from flagged websites as “fake” and every Reuters article as “true”. The model learns that contrast.
        • It is overconfident on unfamiliar writing. Calibration holds on the training distribution (ECE 0.2%) but not elsewhere (ECE 19%).
        • Narrow time and topic window. Mostly US politics and world news from 2015–2018.
        • English only. Non-English text is mostly outside the vocabulary and triggers a warning.
        • Easy to game. Rewriting a false story in wire-service style will lower its score.
        • Use it to ask better questions. Who is quoted? Why so many superlatives? Where is the primary source? The score can prompt those questions; it cannot settle them.
